Plates of Doubt Contains motion & flicker

Seeing
is negotiable.

Nine experiments on the one organ
you were never meant to question.

Move your cursor. The pattern under it is not what you think it is.

Begin ↓
I

Peripheral drift

Rotating snakes

Nothing here rotates. Each ring is a fixed run of four tones — black, blue, white, yellow — always in the same order. Your visual system reads that luminance ramp as motion, and every tiny eye movement feeds it.

Look beside a disc, not at it. The ones in the corner of your vision move the most.

Click a disc to drain its colour. The motion dies with it, because the effect needs the specific luminance steps, not the hue.

II

Motion adaptation

The waterfall that stops

Neurons that detect expansion get tired. Stare at the centre of the spiral for twenty seconds and they stop answering. When it halts, their quiet opposites win the argument — and a motionless picture appears to crawl backwards.

Keep your eyes on the dot. Do not follow the arms.

When it freezes, look at this paragraph.

III

Café wall

Parallel, every line

The grey lines are perfectly horizontal and perfectly parallel. Offset the rows by half a tile, put high-contrast tiles against a mid-grey seam, and the lines buckle into wedges.

Drag the offset to a full tile. The pattern becomes an ordinary chessboard and the tilt disappears.

IV

Lateral inhibition

Dots that aren't

There are no dark spots anywhere on this grid. Retinal cells compare each point with its surroundings; at an intersection, more of the surround is bright, so the centre is judged darker than the streets.

In the scintillating version the dots flash as your gaze jumps — and refuse to appear wherever you look directly.

V

Lightness constancy

A is B

Square A and square B are the same grey. Not similar — identical. Your brain knows a shadow lies across B, quietly subtracts it, and hands you a paler square than the pixels contain.

Don't take our word for it. Click A, then B, and read the real pixel values off the canvas.

VI

Afterimage · Troxler fading

Lilac chaser

Fixate the cross and don't move your eyes. A gap runs around the ring — then a green disc appears in it, then the pink vanishes entirely and only the green disc keeps circling.

The green is an afterimage, the complement of the pink. The vanishing is your visual system discarding anything that holds still in the periphery. Neither exists on screen.

VII

Illusory contours

The triangle nobody drew

Three notched discs and three open angles. Your brain refuses to believe that is a coincidence, invents a white triangle lying on top, paints it brighter than the page, and draws crisp edges where there is no ink.

VIII

Depth misjudgement

Shepard's tables

The two tabletops are the same parallelogram — one is simply the other turned by ninety degrees. But the legs tell your brain that the left one recedes into depth, so it stretches it into a long, narrow plank.

IX

Ambiguity

The cube with no front

A wireframe has no near side. Every edge is the same weight, so the geometry fits two equally good interpretations — and your brain swaps between them every few seconds, reversing the direction of rotation with no input from you.

Add a depth cue and one reading wins permanently. Thicker lines are nearer.
